Getting Your License

How to earn a Private Pilot certificate: requirements, the medical exam, ground school, flight training, and both FAA tests.

For thousands of years, people have dreamt about flying, but it wasn't until the Wright brothers at Kitty Hawk that we finally took to the air. Earning your license lets you take joy rides with friends and family, fly yourself to business meetings, and become a true student of the sky. Here's how to get there.

Requirements

You must be at least 16 to earn your student pilot certificate and fly solo (you can start lessons earlier, so you're ready to solo on your 16th birthday). You must be 17 to receive the regular pilot certificate. There is no upper age limit.

You must be in acceptable health — nearly everyone passes the required medical exam, and glasses are allowed during the eye exam. You must also be able to read, speak, and understand English, the international language of aviation.

The License (Certificate)

Everyone calls it a "license," but the FAA's technical term is certificate. A Private Pilot certificate lets you fly a single-engine airplane under Visual Flight Rules (at least 3 miles visibility), including at night. You may carry passengers, but not for compensation — though you may split costs.

From here you can work toward certificates for commercial flying or instructing, and add ratings for multi-engine aircraft, instrument flight, or different aircraft categories like helicopters, seaplanes, balloons, and gliders.

Step 1

Get Medically Certified

Before soloing, you'll need to be examined by an FAA-designated Airman Medical Examiner (ask your flight school for a recommendation, or search ame.cami.jccbi.gov). The exam typically costs $125 and covers a questionnaire plus tests of equilibrium, nose, throat, hearing, and vision.

Your third-class airman medical certificate is valid for 5 years if you're under 40, and 2 years if you're older. A serious medical condition doesn't necessarily disqualify you — disclose everything to your examiner. Once certified, you'll apply for your Student Pilot Certificate, which involves a 2–3 week TSA background check.

Step 2

Ground School

You'll study flight regulations, aerodynamics, radio communications, navigation, airport operations, physiology, weather reports, aircraft systems, and weather theory — the core knowledge of flying. Golden State's ground school is an 11-week course meeting every Tuesday and Thursday night at 6:30 PM, for $455.

Step 3

Train in the Air

You'll fly 30+ hours with your instructor plus solo practice. Each lesson runs about two hours (roughly one hour in the air, plus pre- and post-flight briefing). Most students take 55–70 hours total. Some finish in under 30 days; most take about five months.

Step 4

Fly Solo

Early in training, your instructor steps out of the plane and lets you fly by yourself — a milestone moment. From there you can practice and hone your skills solo, without paying for instruction while you build hours.

Step 5

Take the Written Test

Golden State is FAA-approved to administer the airman knowledge test on-site. You'll have 2.5 hours to answer 60 multiple-choice questions and need 70% to pass. Once you pass, you have two years to complete the in-flight practical test.

Final Step

The Flight Test (Check Ride)

You'll meet an FAA examiner, who first asks questions vocally for 1–2 hours after you provide a written recommendation from your flight instructor. Then you'll both fly, and the examiner evaluates how you handle the aircraft. Pass, and you're issued a temporary pilot certificate; your permanent certificate arrives by mail within a couple of months. Your license is good for life and never expires.
