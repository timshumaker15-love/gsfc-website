Become a Career Pilot

“If you enjoy what you do, you'll never work another day in your life.”

Pilots are in high demand across the industry, with a shortage both in the short term and looming over the decades ahead. An aging workforce, FAA-mandated retirements, and steadily growing demand for air travel are creating a strong market for pilots — with rising salaries and available bonuses. If you're considering a career as a professional airline pilot, there's rarely been a better time to start.

The first step for every career pilot is the same: earn your Private Pilot License. From there you build hours and ratings toward a Commercial Certificate. If you're eyeing a four-year aeronautical program, it's usually smart to earn your Private Pilot license first — doing it at the school itself can cost three times as much.

Student Story

From Golden State CFI to First Officer with SkyWest

Glenn worked full-time as a flight instructor at Golden State Flying Club, building flight hours along the way. He's now a First Officer with SkyWest Airlines. The path wasn't a straight line — during an economic downturn, Glenn tried working as a 911 dispatcher and found it wasn't for him. He came back to Golden State, kept building hours, and eventually earned the airline job he was after.

Glenn's path is a common one: earn your Private Pilot license, become a Certified Flight Instructor, and get paid to build the hours needed to qualify for an airline. Outside of prior military pilots, most airline pilots come up through exactly this route rather than spending hundreds of thousands of dollars at a dedicated aviation school.

Story originally published November 2021.

Industry Context

A long-running pilot shortage, and a slow generational shift

Since the mid-2000s, the number of 20–35 year old pilots has been slowly climbing back up after decades of decline, helping offset a fall in other age groups. There are still tens of thousands fewer pilots today than at the industry's peak, and roughly a fifth of certified U.S. commercial pilots are over 50 — against a mandatory retirement age of 65. Smaller regional and cargo carriers, in particular, continue to struggle to fill cockpits even with rising starting pay.

The barriers for new pilots remain largely the same: training cost, and a historically narrow recruiting pipeline that hasn't fully reflected the diversity of newer generations. Golden State's pay-as-you-go structure and CFI-hours pathway exist specifically to make that first step — the Private Pilot license — more attainable.

Background reporting referenced from Axios (April 2019) and coverage of Cal Fire's aerial firefighting pilot shortage (The Sacramento Bee, September 2018).
